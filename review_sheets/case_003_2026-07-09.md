## Must-not-miss rule-out reasoning
**Subarachnoid Haemorrhage (SAH)** — Excluded
  - _hx_: Headache is progressive over 5 days — SAH classically presents as 'thunderclap' (worst headache of life, maximal within seconds of onset, not gradually worsening)
  - _inv_: CT head: no subarachnoid blood in cisterns or sulci — CT within 6 hours has ~95% sensitivity for SAH
  - _inv_: Lumbar puncture: CSF colourless and clear, no xanthochromia (yellow staining from blood breakdown products), no red blood cells — excludes SAH definitively

**Meningitis** — Ruled out
  - _hx_: No fever, no recent infection, no immunosuppression — classic risk factors for bacterial meningitis absent
  - _ex_: No neck stiffness (Kernig's and Brudzinski's signs negative). No photophobia. No purpuric rash on skin examination.
  - _inv_: Normal CRP and white cell count — bacterial meningitis causes a marked systemic inflammatory response; normal inflammatory markers make it very unlikely
  - _inv_: CSF analysis: clear fluid, normal glucose ratio (>0.6), normal protein, no organisms on Gram stain, no white cells — excludes bacterial and viral meningitis

**Ischaemic Stroke** — Excluded — this is venous, not arterial
  - _hx_: Gradual onset headache over 5 days before focal deficit — arterial stroke typically causes sudden onset focal deficit without days of prodromal headache
  - _inv_: CT head: no arterial territory infarct. MRI DWI: no restricted diffusion in an arterial territory — rules out arterial ischaemic stroke
  - _inv_: MR venography: absent flow signal in superior sagittal sinus and right transverse sinus — confirms venous (not arterial) thrombosis as the cause of the focal deficit

**Hypertensive Emergency** — Ruled out
  - _ex_: Blood pressure 134/86 mmHg — elevated but not in hypertensive emergency range (>180/120 mmHg with end-organ damage)
  - _ex_: No papilloedema on fundoscopy attributable to hypertension — the papilloedema here is from raised ICP due to venous obstruction, not hypertensive retinopathy
  - _hx_: No prior history of hypertension. Young patient. Mild BP elevation is likely reactive to pain and raised ICP rather than the primary cause.

## Key teaching points
1. Cerebral Venous Sinus Thrombosis (CVST) is a blood clot in the large veins that drain blood from the brain. Unlike arterial stroke, it typically presents with a progressively worsening headache over days, making it easy to miss and misdiagnose as migraine.
   _Clinician: ☐ accurate  ☐ needs change_
2. The oral contraceptive pill (OCP) is the most important risk factor for CVST in young women. Oestrogen in the combined OCP increases clotting tendency (prothrombotic effect) by 3-6 times. Any young woman on OCP presenting with an unusual or progressive headache should have CVST considered.

## Approach insight
CVST is the classic 'dangerous diagnosis masquerading as a primary headache.' It presents with a progressive headache that can be misdiagnosed as migraine for days. The RED FLAGS that should prompt urgent investigation are: (1) Progressive worsening despite treatment, (2) New focal neurological deficit, (3) OCP use or other prothrombotic risk factors — identified in drug history, (4) Papilloedema on fundoscopy. The critical lesson: migraine and tension headache are diagnoses of EXCLUSION — you must first rule out all dangerous secondary causes.

## Expert narrative
CVST in a young woman on the combined OCP is the classic presentation. Oestrogen in the OCP increases clotting factor activity, reducing natural anticoagulant protein S activity and raising the risk of venous thrombosis by 3-6 times. Thrombosis of the superior sagittal sinus and transverse sinus obstructs venous outflow from the brain, causing raised intracranial pressure (explaining the progressive headache and papilloedema) and cortical venous congestion/ischaemia (explaining the focal left arm weakness from right parietal lobe involvement). The key diagnostic insight: unlike SAH (thunderclap, maximal at onset) or arterial stroke (sudden focal deficit, no headache prodrome), CVST is subacute and progressive over days. It requires MR venography for diagnosis — a normal CT head does not exclude it. Treatment is anticoagulation. The antiphospholipid antibody screen was positive here, identifying an additional prothrombotic risk factor that was amplified by OCP use.

## Sensitivity note
CVST is poorly sensitive for a 'classic' headache presentation — it is subacute, progressive, and mimics migraine. Headache occurs in ~90% of cases but its character is variable. A high index of suspicion is required in young women on OCP with unusual or progressive headache. CT head and LP may be normal — MR venography is the essential investigation.

## Specificity note
MR venography showing absent flow in a dural venous sinus (e.g. superior sagittal sinus, transverse sinus) is highly specific for CVST. The 'empty delta sign' on CT with contrast (filling defect in the sinus where there should be enhancing flowing blood, surrounded by enhancing walls) is also specific but less sensitive (~30%). D-dimer elevation supports the diagnosis but is not specific.
