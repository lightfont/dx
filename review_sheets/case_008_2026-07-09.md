## Approach
Acute generalised weakness is localised by its company: areflexia and an ascending pattern point to the peripheral nerve (GBS); a sensory level and brisk reflexes to the cord; fatigability to the neuromuscular junction; and episodic, potassium-linked attacks to muscle. Whatever the cause, the airway and autonomic status come first.

## Approach insight
Two things must happen at once in ascending weakness: localise, and protect the airway. Localisation turns on a few cheap findings — areflexia and a symmetric ascending pattern place the lesion in the peripheral nerve and make GBS the front-runner, while a discrete sensory level, brisk reflexes or early sphincter loss would redirect urgently to the cord. The reversible mimics are actively excluded (potassium for periodic paralysis; the descending pattern and pupils for botulism). But the examination that cannot wait is respiratory: the single-breath count and serial FVC are tracked from the outset because neuromuscular respiratory failure arrives before the oxygen saturation falls, and autonomic instability can kill through arrhythmia. CSF and nerve conduction studies confirm GBS, and an MRI of the cord is the safety net that retires the surgical mimic before the label is applied.

## Expert narrative
Hearing 'ascending weakness over days after a diarrhoeal illness', the clinician is on GBS at once — but the examination is run to confirm the localisation and, above all, to gauge the airway. Areflexia and the absence of a sensory level place the lesion in the peripheral nerves and steer firmly away from a cord lesion; normal pupils and an ascending direction argue against botulism; a normal potassium retires periodic paralysis. In parallel — not afterwards — the respiratory reserve is measured with a single-breath count and serial FVC, and the patient is put on a cardiac monitor for autonomic instability, because both can deteriorate faster than the saturation reading suggests. Only then does the work-up move to confirmation: a lumbar puncture for albuminocytological dissociation (accepting it may be normal in the first week), nerve conduction studies to prove and subtype the demyelination, and an MRI of the spine to make certain no compressive lesion has been missed.

## Sensitivity note
CSF albuminocytological dissociation is time-dependent — protein is raised in roughly half of patients in the first days but the large majority by two weeks, so a normal early lumbar puncture does not exclude GBS. Nerve conduction studies are highly sensitive for the neuropathy but may be subtle very early. Serial FVC is the most sensitive practical marker of impending respiratory failure — far more so than oxygen saturation.

## Specificity note
Areflexia with an ascending, symmetric flaccid weakness after an antecedent infection is highly suggestive of GBS, and albuminocytological dissociation with a demyelinating NCS pattern is specific in the right context. A discrete sensory level is specific for a cord lesion, a normal potassium specifically excludes hypokalaemic periodic paralysis, and dilated unreactive pupils with descending weakness would specifically suggest botulism.
