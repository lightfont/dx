## Approach
A new headache after 50 is never dismissed as primary until the dangerous secondary causes are excluded. The sieve runs through vasculitis (GCA), bleeding (SAH), clot (stroke/TIA), raised pressure/mass and the eye (angle-closure), with primary headaches a diagnosis of exclusion.

## Approach insight
The whole game in this presentation is time-to-steroids. Giant cell arteritis announces itself through a cluster of features — new temporal headache, scalp tenderness, jaw claudication, girdle stiffness and, most ominously, transient visual loss — and the examination adds a tender, pulseless temporal artery and, if you look, a pale swollen disc. ESR and CRP are the fast confirmatory pivot, and the temporal artery biopsy (or an ultrasound halo sign) seals it. But the teaching point that outranks all others is that you do NOT wait for the biopsy: once GCA is suspected, high-dose steroids are started immediately to save sight, and the biopsy — which stays positive for weeks — is done afterwards. The parallel discipline is to keep the other dangerous causes live until excluded: a thunderclap onset would force a SAH work-up, a focal deficit would force imaging for stroke or a mass.

## Expert narrative
Confronted with a new headache in a 72-year-old, the clinician's first instinct is GCA, and the consultation is built around proving it fast enough to save vision. The history deliberately seeks the high-yield features — jaw claudication (the most specific), amaurosis fugax (the most dangerous), scalp tenderness and PMR-type stiffness — while screening out the thunderclap and focal-deficit stories that would redirect to SAH or stroke. The examination palpates the temporal artery and inspects the optic disc, and ESR/CRP are sent immediately. The crucial move, taught but not simulated here, is to start high-dose corticosteroids the moment GCA is suspected rather than waiting for histology, because the biopsy stays positive for weeks but the optic nerve does not wait. Ultrasound (halo sign) and biopsy then confirm, and CT excludes the structural mimics. The skill rewarded is recognising a sight-threatening emergency from a cluster of ordinary-sounding symptoms and not letting confirmatory testing delay treatment.

## Sensitivity note
ESR and CRP together are highly sensitive — a normal ESR AND CRP makes GCA unlikely — but neither is specific. Temporal artery biopsy is specific but can miss disease through skip lesions (sampling error), so a negative biopsy does not exclude GCA when the clinical picture and ultrasound are supportive.

## Specificity note
Jaw claudication and a tender, pulseless temporal artery are relatively specific clinical features, and biopsy-proven granulomatous arteritis is definitive. The ultrasound halo sign is a specific imaging adjunct. Raised inflammatory markers alone are non-specific and must be interpreted within the weighted 2022 criteria and the clinical context.
