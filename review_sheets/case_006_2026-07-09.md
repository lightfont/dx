## Approach
Epigastric pain radiating to the back has a short list of causes that kill (aortic dissection, MI, perforation, mesenteric ischaemia) and a short list that are common (pancreatitis, peptic ulcer, biliary disease). Pancreatitis is diagnosed cheaply and early with pain + lipase; the harder skill is sequencing the imaging — duct before ERCP, CT deferred.

## Approach insight
There are two separate skills this case examines. The first is the differential of epigastric/back pain, where the discipline is to screen the vascular and cardiac killers (dissection, MI) and the biliary emergency (cholangitis) before settling on the common answer. The second — and the one most often done badly — is investigation order. Pancreatitis is a clinical–biochemical diagnosis: characteristic pain plus a lipase >3× ULN meets Revised Atlanta, so a CT is not needed to make the diagnosis and is actively misleading if used early to gauge necrosis. The aetiology and the duct are then assessed by ultrasound, and a suspected duct stone WITHOUT cholangitis is chased with MRCP or EUS — never a diagnostic ERCP, which can ignite the very pancreatitis you are treating. ERCP earns its place only when a stone is obstructing or cholangitis is present.

## Expert narrative
Confronted with two days of boring epigastric pain through to the back, the experienced clinician makes the diagnosis quickly and cheaply — send a lipase, and with a typical history a level over three times normal closes it under Revised Atlanta. The energy then goes two ways. First, to the killers that share this pain: a focused history and examination screen for dissection and MI, and if suspicion is real the aorta is imaged with CT — because, as the examination notes, equal arm pressures do not exclude a dissection. Second, to severity and aetiology: a Glasgow score is assembled from the bloods and gas, fluids are run hard, and an ultrasound finds the gallstones and reads the duct. The discriminating judgement is what NOT to do — not to order an early CT expecting it to grade necrosis, and above all not to send the patient for a diagnostic ERCP. With no cholangitis and an unobstructed duct, the duct is interrogated non-invasively by MRCP or EUS, and ERCP is held in reserve. The case rewards the student who diagnoses on lipase, stratifies on Glasgow, and sequences the imaging correctly.

## Sensitivity note
Lipase is more sensitive and specific than amylase and remains elevated longer, making it the test of choice; however the magnitude of elevation does not correlate with severity. CT is insensitive for necrosis in the first 72 hours, so an early normal-looking scan is falsely reassuring — severity is judged clinically and by score, not by early CT. MRCP and EUS are both sensitive for choledocholithiasis, with EUS superior for small stones and microlithiasis.

## Specificity note
A lipase >3× the upper limit of normal with characteristic pain is specific enough to diagnose acute pancreatitis without imaging. Charcot's triad is specific for cholangitis but insensitive — its absence does not fully exclude a duct stone, which is why the duct is imaged when LFTs are deranged. A normal CT aortogram is definitive for excluding dissection, which clinical signs cannot do.
