A definite diagnosis needs A + B + C; A + B alone is a suspected diagnosis.

## Approach
Right-upper-quadrant pain with fever is biliary until proven otherwise, but the job is to place it precisely: is this the gallbladder (cholecystitis), the duct (stone/cholangitis), the liver (hepatitis), the pancreas, or a referred cardiac/pleural cause? The discriminators are jaundice, the liver panel pattern, the lipase and the ultrasound.

## Approach insight
The trap in right-upper-quadrant pain is to stop at 'gallstones' and miss either the dangerous duct (cholangitis) or a referred killer (inferior MI). The clean way through is to ask three questions in order: is the duct obstructed (jaundice, dark urine, cholestatic panel, dilated CBD)? — if not, cholangitis and a retained stone fall away. Is the pancreas involved (back radiation, lipase >3× ULN)? — if not, pancreatitis falls away. Is this referred from the chest (ECG, troponin, chest film)? — if not, the heart and lung fall away. What remains, anchored by a positive Murphy's sign and a confirmatory ultrasound, is acute cholecystitis. Crucially, the absence of duct obstruction is also what tells you ERCP has no role — there is nothing to clear.

## Expert narrative
The experienced surgeon hears 'days of constant RUQ pain with fever on a background of fatty-food colic' and is already on cholecystitis, but spends the consultation deliberately excluding the things that change management. Jaundice and a cholestatic panel are hunted because cholangitis is the emergency that needs the duct cleared; a lipase is sent because gallstone pancreatitis sits one stone away; an ECG and troponin are done because an inferior MI hides in this exact location. The ultrasound is then used to confirm the gallbladder and — just as importantly — to read the bile duct. With a normal bilirubin, a non-dilated duct and no ductal stone, the reasoning closes: this is acute cholecystitis, and there is nothing for an ERCP to do. The discipline is to let the cheap, fast tests (lipase, LFT, ECG, ultrasound) retire each dangerous mimic rather than jumping to invasive imaging.

## Sensitivity note
Ultrasound is highly sensitive for gallstones and, combined with wall thickening, pericholecystic fluid and a sonographic Murphy's sign, is sensitive for acute cholecystitis; a normal study in a convincing clinical picture should still prompt further imaging (CT or HIDA). Bedside Murphy's sign is sensitive but less specific. A normal bilirubin and non-dilated CBD are reassuring but do not completely exclude a small or passed ductal stone — which is why MRCP/EUS exist for the equivocal case.

## Specificity note
A positive sonographic Murphy's sign with gallbladder wall thickening and pericholecystic fluid is highly specific for acute cholecystitis. Charcot's triad is specific for cholangitis but insensitive (present in a minority); its absence does not fully exclude the diagnosis, so the liver panel and duct calibre are checked. A lipase >3× the upper limit of normal is specific for pancreatitis and would change the diagnosis entirely.
